The Quiet Art of the Quill

On four generations of cinnamon peelers in the Matale hills

At dawn the bark is still damp, and that is the point. A peeler named Sunil works a brass rod beneath the outer skin of a two-year branch, lifting it away in a single unbroken sheet no thicker than a sheet of paper. The inner bark — pale, fragrant, almost sweet to the touch — is what becomes cinnamon.

It cannot be rushed. Cut too early and the quill tears; too late and the oils dull. The window is a matter of days, read by eye and by smell, a judgement that takes years to learn and a lifetime to perfect. "My grandfather could tell a good tree by the sound of the rod," Sunil says, not looking up from his work.

This is why true Ceylon cinnamon costs what it does, and why it tastes the way it does — like citrus and honey rather than the hot, flat sweetness of the cassia that fills most supermarket jars. You are not buying a spice. You are buying a morning's patience, multiplied across four generations.
